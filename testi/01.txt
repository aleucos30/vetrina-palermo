Cattedrale di Palermo
Benvenuti davanti alla Cattedrale di Palermo, dedicata a Maria Assunta. Alzate lo sguardo e noterete subito la mescolanza di stili. La chiesa fu fondata nel 1184 dall'arcivescovo Gualtiero Offamilio, nel luogo in cui sorgeva un edificio più antico che, durante il dominio arabo, era stato trasformato in moschea. Dell'epoca normanna restano le absidi, mentre il portico meridionale, con i suoi archi gotici, risale al Quattrocento. La grande cupola è molto più tarda: la realizzò l'architetto Ferdinando Fuga alla fine del Settecento, dando all'interno un aspetto neoclassico. Dentro, in grandi sarcofagi di porfido, riposano sovrani che hanno fatto la storia della Sicilia, tra cui Ruggero II e l'imperatore Federico II. Per questo la cattedrale è come un libro di pietra che racconta otto secoli di dominazioni diverse, e oggi fa parte del patrimonio mondiale dell'Unesco, nell'itinerario della Palermo arabo-normanna.

Si racconta che nel 1624, mentre la peste devastava Palermo, sul Monte Pellegrino fossero ritrovate le ossa di Rosalia, una giovane eremita vissuta nel dodicesimo secolo. I resti furono portati in processione per le strade della città e, secondo la tradizione, il contagio cessò. Da allora Santa Rosalia, che i palermitani chiamano affettuosamente la Santuzza, è la patrona della città. La sua urna d'argento è custodita proprio qui, in cattedrale, e ogni anno a metà luglio Palermo le dedica il Festino, la festa più sentita dell'anno.
## en
Palermo Cathedral
Welcome to Palermo Cathedral, dedicated to the Assumption of Mary. Look up, and you will immediately notice how many styles are mixed together. The church was founded in 1184 by Archbishop Walter of the Mill, on the site of an older building that had been turned into a mosque during the Arab period. The apses date back to Norman times, while the southern porch, with its Gothic arches, was added in the fifteenth century. The great dome came much later: the architect Ferdinando Fuga built it at the end of the eighteenth century, giving the interior its neoclassical look. Inside, in enormous porphyry sarcophagi, rest rulers who shaped the history of Sicily, among them Roger the Second and the emperor Frederick the Second. That is why the cathedral is like a book written in stone, telling the story of eight centuries of different rulers. Today it belongs to the World Heritage site of Arab-Norman Palermo.

Legend has it that in 1624, while the plague was ravaging Palermo, the bones of Rosalia, a young hermit who had lived in the twelfth century, were found on Monte Pellegrino. The remains were carried in procession through the streets, and according to tradition, the epidemic came to an end. Since then Saint Rosalia, whom the people of Palermo fondly call the Santuzza, has been the patron saint of the city. Her silver urn is kept right here in the cathedral, and every year in mid July the city celebrates her with the Festino, its most beloved festival.

The Norman Palace and the Palatine Chapel
You are standing in front of the Norman Palace, considered one of the oldest royal residences in Europe, and still the seat of the Sicilian Regional Assembly today. Here, on foundations of Arab origin, the Norman kings set up the heart of their kingdom. The jewel of the palace is the Palatine Chapel, commissioned by Roger the Second, who became king of Sicily in 1130, and consecrated in 1140. When you step inside, you will be speechless. The walls and the dome are covered with golden mosaics made by Byzantine masters, with Christ Pantocrator gazing down at you from above. The wooden ceiling, carved and painted in the shape of stalactites, is the work of Islamic craftsmen, while the plan of the building follows the Latin tradition. In this small space three cultures meet, and you can even read inscriptions in Greek, in Latin and in Arabic. It was the private church of the king and his court, designed to show the wealth and power of the new kingdom.

Here is something curious. The ceiling of the Palatine Chapel is painted with a great many figures showing scenes of court life, musicians, dancers, hunters and animals, in the style of Islamic art. These are surprising images for a church, and they show how open the Norman court of Palermo was to Arab culture. The Arab geographer al-Idrisi even worked at the court of Roger the Second, and made a famous world map and a book of geography for the king.

The Church of the Martorana
This is the church of Saint Mary of the Admiral, which everyone calls the Martorana. It was founded in 1143 by George of Antioch, admiral of King Roger the Second, and you can recognize it at once by its Norman bell tower, one of the most elegant in the city. The name Martorana came later, in 1433, when a noblewoman, Eloisa Martorana, founded a Benedictine convent next door, to which the church was later entrusted. Over the centuries the building has been transformed, and the facade and much of the interior now have a Baroque look. But as soon as you approach the altar, you will find its ancient heart, with splendid Byzantine mosaics on a golden background. In one, King Roger the Second is crowned directly by Christ. In another, Admiral George bows down at the feet of the Virgin. These images tell us a great deal about how the Norman kings wanted to be seen: rulers chosen by God, surrounded by loyal men.

Legend has it that the nuns of the Martorana convent, on the eve of a very important visit, saw that their garden was bare, and so they shaped fake fruit out of almond paste, colored like the real thing, and hung it on the trees. This story is said to be the origin of frutta martorana, the typical sweet of Palermo, which still today imitates figs, pears, oranges and lemons to perfection. It is made above all around the commemoration of the dead, at the beginning of November.

## en
The Church of San Cataldo
In front of you, in Piazza Bellini, stands one of the most recognizable churches in Palermo: San Cataldo, with its three red domes and its walls of cut stone, decorated near the top with battlements and blind arches. It was built around 1154 by Maio of Bari, the powerful grand admiral of King William the First, and it is a perfect example of the Arab-Norman style. From outside it looks austere and compact, like a small fortress. Inside you will find no mosaics on the walls, because the decoration of the church was never completed. What remains is a floor inlaid with colored marble, simple ancient columns and a peaceful, luminous atmosphere. In the nineteenth century the building, after several different uses, even housed the post office, and was then restored to bring back its Norman appearance. Today it is part of the World Heritage route of Arab-Norman Palermo, and it is entrusted to the Equestrian Order of the Holy Sepulchre of Jerusalem.

Here is something curious. Maio of Bari, the man who had the church built, never saw it finished. He was killed in 1160 in a conspiracy of nobles led by Matteo Bonello, and from that moment the work stopped. Maio was one of the most powerful men at court, and he was much disputed by the barons of the kingdom. That is why the interior of San Cataldo looks so plain today, almost severe, and the church remains a beautiful work left unfinished.

Around you is one of the most evocative places in Palermo: the church of San Giovanni degli Eremiti, with its five red domes standing out against the greenery. It was built around 1136 at the wish of Roger the Second and entrusted to Benedictine monks. The style is Arab-Norman: a simple, austere plan, cube shaped volumes topped by domes, and narrow, tall windows. Next to the church opens a small cloister with paired columns, where a garden of citrus trees, Mediterranean plants and palm trees grows. In the nineteenth century the buildings added over the centuries were removed, to give the monument back its medieval appearance and to show off the domes. Today it is among the monuments of Arab-Norman Palermo on the World Heritage list, and it offers one of the quietest and most photographed corners of the city.

Legend has it that before the Normans, a monastery founded by Pope Gregory the Great stood on this spot, and that during the Arab period the building was turned into a mosque. Near the church you can still see the remains of a small room, which tradition points to as part of that ancient mosque. If that is true, this little corner tells the story of Palermo in just a few meters: first Christian, then Muslim, and finally Christian again with the arrival of the Normans.
